You're bone tired. You dragged yourself to bed and now you're staring at the ceiling, wide awake. If you've typed "can't sleep but exhausted" into a search bar at midnight, this is probably the night that sent you there: your brain running through tomorrow's meeting, last week's conversation, and approximately seventeen things you forgot to do.

Being unable to sleep but exhausted is one of the most frustrating experiences there is, partly because it feels like your body is breaking a basic rule. You did everything right: you're tired, you're in bed, it's late. Why won't it work?

The short answer is that exhaustion and sleep readiness are not the same thing. Your body can be depleted while your brain stays switched on. Once you understand why that happens, the fixes become more obvious.

## Key takeaways

- Being tired tells your body you need sleep. It does not automatically switch off the alerting systems that keep you awake.
- Stress hormones and mental hyperarousal can override physical exhaustion and prevent sleep onset.
- The most useful responses tend to be counterintuitive: getting out of bed, writing things down, keeping the room cooler, and not trying harder to fall asleep.

## Why you can't sleep even when you're exhausted

There's a name for what you're experiencing: hyperarousal. It refers to a state where your brain's wakefulness systems stay activated, and [research identifies it as a central factor in insomnia](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7226089/), capable of preventing sleep onset even when the body is physically run down.

Three things often feed it at once.

**Mental hyperarousal** is the replay loop: tomorrow's worries, today's unfinished business, the low-level hum of things left undone. The mind treats unresolved problems as items that still need attention. It keeps running them not out of anxiety alone, but because nothing has signaled that they've been dealt with.

**Physiological hyperarousal** is what happens in your body. [According to NIH research](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK279071/), wakefulness is associated with elevated cortisol levels, while deep slow-wave sleep is associated with cortisol declining. In people who struggle with insomnia, this stress hormone system can remain elevated across the whole day and night, which may explain both the daytime fatigue and the difficulty falling asleep. The exhaustion and the inability to sleep come from the same place.

**Conditioned hyperarousal** builds over time. When you've spent many nights lying awake in bed, your nervous system starts to associate the bedroom itself with wakefulness. Getting into bed can start triggering alertness instead of drowsiness.

[Stress is one of the main drivers of this cycle](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/what-causes-insomnia): it produces a physical response that contributes to hyperarousal, and then the inability to sleep becomes its own stressor, making the next night harder too.

## What makes it worse

A few things people try instinctively backfire.

Watching the clock is a reliable way to increase sleep anxiety. Every glance is a calculation ("if I fall asleep right now I'll get five hours and twenty minutes") that raises cortisol and reinforces the wakefulness loop.

Trying hard to sleep also backfires. Sleep is one of the few things that gets harder the more effort you direct at it. Actively trying to force unconsciousness tends to create exactly the kind of alertness that blocks it.

Staying in bed for hours hoping sleep will eventually come trains your nervous system to treat bed as a place where you lie awake. Over time this makes the problem worse rather than better.

## 7 things to try tonight

### 1. Turn the clock away (or out of sight)

If your phone or a clock is visible from bed, flip it or put it face-down. Knowing you can't check the time removes one of the most reliable anxiety triggers of a sleepless night. If you use your phone as an alarm, set it and put it across the room.

### 2. Get out of bed if you've been awake for more than 20 minutes

This is one of the core principles in CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia), which [the Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/treatment/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-insomnia) identifies as the recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia. The idea: go somewhere dim and quiet and do something calm until you feel genuinely sleepy, then return. You're trying to rebuild the association between bed and sleep, rather than between bed and lying awake.

This feels counterintuitive. It works for many people precisely because it breaks the conditioned wakefulness cycle.

### 3. Write down what's on your mind

Get a notepad or your phone and write out the things that are running through your head. Two variations depending on what the noise is:

If it's tomorrow's tasks and things you might forget, write a short to-do list. A [2018 Baylor University study](https://news.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2018/can-writing-your-dos-help-you-doze-baylor-study-suggests-jotting-down-tasks-can) (published in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 57 participants) found that people who wrote a to-do list for the next day fell asleep about nine minutes faster than those who wrote about what they'd already done. Forward-looking writing seems to signal to the brain that tomorrow is handled.

If it's worries or racing thoughts, write those down too. Getting them out of your head and onto a page is a way of telling your nervous system they've been registered, without requiring you to solve them.

The [bedtime journal](/blog/bedtime-journal) approach goes further, but even a few minutes of freewriting tonight costs nothing. If you're too tired to write, meow note lets you speak your thoughts into your phone in the dark, which requires almost no effort at all.

### 4. Make the room cooler and darker

A cooler, darker room creates better conditions for sleep onset for most people. If you can lower the temperature a few degrees, open a window, or add a fan, try it. Blackout curtains or a sleep mask help if light is coming in. These are environmental adjustments, not cures, but they remove friction.

### 5. Breathe out for longer than you breathe in

Breathing slowly with a longer exhale than inhale is a technique many people find calming when they're lying awake. A simple version: inhale for four counts, exhale slowly for six or eight. You don't need a script. The point is to make the out-breath noticeably longer than the in-breath and do that for a few minutes. Many people find this easier to focus on than trying to "relax," because it gives the mind something concrete to track.

### 6. Do something quiet and screen-free if you get up

If you're out of bed waiting to feel sleepy again, avoid anything that engages you too much. Bright screens and interesting content work against you. Useful options: reading something undemanding in very dim light, sitting quietly, light stretching, or listening to something slow and familiar (a podcast you've heard before, ambient sound). The goal is quiet wakefulness while your sleep drive builds.

### 7. Commit to the same wake time tomorrow

This is the one that works over time. Consistent wake times build sleep pressure across the day. When you sleep in to compensate for a bad night, you push the problem into tomorrow. Setting an alarm and getting up at the same time, even after a rough night, is uncomfortable but it helps recalibrate the system faster than most other things. It's the foundation of what makes [CBT-I](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/treatment/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-insomnia) effective.

## Building a wind-down that actually works

Most of the fixes above are reactive, what to do when you're already awake and frustrated. A consistent wind-down routine addresses the problem earlier, before you get into bed.

Forty-five minutes to an hour before bed: lower lights, put screens away or at least into a night mode, and do something that moves you away from active engagement. For people who carry a lot from the day, [journaling for sleep](/blog/journaling-for-sleep) is one of the more practical options because it gives the to-do list and the worry loop somewhere to land before you get horizontal.

If you're someone who often lies awake with a busy mind, [how to stop overthinking at night](/blog/how-to-stop-overthinking-at-night) covers the mental side in more detail. And if what wakes you (or keeps you awake) is closer to a physical rush of dread, [rush of anxiety when falling asleep](/blog/rush-of-anxiety-when-falling-asleep) covers that specifically.

## When to talk to someone

If you've been unable to sleep despite exhaustion for three months or more, if it's affecting how you function during the day, if it's accompanied by panic, racing heart, or other physical symptoms, or if you're having thoughts of self-harm, talk to a doctor or mental health professional.

[CBT-I](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/treatment/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-insomnia) is the recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia and is more effective long-term than sleep medication for most people. A GP can refer you or point you toward options in your area.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why can't I sleep even when I'm exhausted?

Physical exhaustion and sleep readiness are separate systems. Exhaustion signals that your body needs rest. But your brain's alerting system, driven by stress hormones and mental activity, can override that signal. This is sometimes called hyperarousal: the wakefulness system stays on despite the fatigue. It's not a personal failure. It's a physiological state that responds to specific interventions.

### How long does the tired-but-wired feeling last?

It varies a lot depending on the cause. If it's situational (a stressful week, an upcoming deadline), it often resolves as circumstances change. If it's become a pattern over months, it may take a few weeks of consistent wind-down habits and wake-time discipline to shift. For persistent cases, working with a sleep specialist or therapist trained in CBT-I typically produces faster results than self-managing.

### Does being exhausted mean you'll sleep badly?

Not necessarily. Exhaustion builds sleep pressure, which is the biological drive to sleep. But it competes with the alerting system. If the alerting system is strong enough, sleep pressure alone won't win. This is why some people sleep much better when they're away from home (less conditioned anxiety) or on vacation (less sustained stress), even though they're not necessarily more tired than usual.

### Is it better to stay in bed or get up?

Get up. Lying awake in bed for long stretches trains your nervous system to associate the bed with wakefulness, which makes falling asleep harder over time. Getting up, doing something calm, and returning only when you're genuinely sleepy is less comfortable in the short term but tends to work better across a few weeks.

### Can what I eat or drink affect this?

Caffeine has a longer half-life than most people expect, and for some people a mid-afternoon coffee is still measurably active at midnight. Alcohol is worth mentioning too: it may help you fall asleep initially but disrupts sleep architecture in the second half of the night, which can leave you waking up tired again. If you suspect either is a factor, cutting off caffeine by 1-2pm and keeping alcohol away from bedtime for a few nights is a reasonable test. These aren't medical recommendations, just reasonable things to try and observe.
