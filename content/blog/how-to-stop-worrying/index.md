Knowing how to stop worrying is easy enough during the day. At night, it's a different problem. The lights go off and your brain starts its nightly broadcast: the email you forgot to send, the conversation that could have gone better, the thing on tomorrow's calendar you've been circling around all week.

Worrying at bedtime isn't a character flaw. It's what happens when the last distraction disappears and your mind finally has space to run. The problem is that it turns your bed into an anxiety hotspot right when your body needs the opposite.

This guide covers seven steps grounded in what research actually shows about worry and sleep, not just what sounds calming. Some of these will feel familiar. A few might surprise you.

## Key takeaways

- Worry disrupts sleep mainly through cognitive arousal, not physical tension, so techniques that target your thinking patterns tend to work better than pure relaxation.
- Scheduling a daily worry window can reduce how much time you spend worrying, though its direct effect on sleep is still being studied.
- Writing a specific to-do list for five minutes at bedtime has been shown in a controlled lab study to help people fall asleep faster.

## Why worrying feels so much worse at night

During the day, tasks, conversations, and movement all compete for your attention. Worry exists but gets interrupted.

At bedtime, the interruptions stop. There's nothing to do, nowhere to go, and no one to talk to. The quiet that's supposed to help you sleep is the same quiet that lets worries surface and grow.

There's a physical piece to this too. When you're anxious, your body releases cortisol and adrenaline, which raise your heart rate and keep your brain alert, according to the [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/mental-health/anxiety-and-sleep). That's useful when you need to respond to a threat. At 11pm in your bedroom, it keeps you awake.

The sleep foundation describes a cycle worth knowing: worry disrupts sleep, and poor sleep makes worry worse the next night. Once that loop is running, it can be hard to step out of without doing something deliberate.

## The worry-sleep link is mostly cognitive, not physical

A [2019 study in Behaviour Therapy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31030873/) tracked 178 adults across two weeks, measuring daily stress, pre-sleep arousal, and sleep quality. The researchers found that people who worried more had stronger connections between daily stress and poor sleep, and that cognitive arousal (racing thoughts, mental replay) accounted for more of the sleep disruption than physical tension did.

That matters for what you try. If progressive muscle relaxation isn't doing much for you on its own, it's not that you're doing it wrong. A tense jaw isn't the main problem for most worriers. The thoughts are.

## 1. Schedule a daily worry window

This is probably the most counterintuitive technique: make an appointment to worry.

Pick a 20-to-30-minute slot earlier in the day, ideally not in the two hours before bed. When a worry shows up outside that window, write it down and tell yourself you'll address it at the scheduled time. Don't push it away. Redirect it.

A [2025 randomized controlled trial published in Psychology & Health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41347618/) found that adding specific "if-then" plans (for example, "if I start worrying at my desk, I will write it down and wait for my 4pm window") reduced worry duration by about 15 minutes per day compared to standard worry postponement alone. The study's participants had 186 people, ran for 14 days, and measured worry duration and frequency daily.

One honest note from that same study: the intervention reduced worry but did not produce significant improvements in sleep onset latency, awakenings, or sleep quality relative to control groups. Scheduling worry time is genuinely useful for managing anxiety during the day. Whether that translates reliably into better sleep is still being studied. Think of it as upstream work, not a bedtime fix by itself.

## 2. Write a specific to-do list before bed

This one has strong sleep-specific evidence behind it.

A [2018 polysomnography study from Baylor University](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29058942/) had 57 healthy adults spend five minutes writing before an overnight sleep lab session. Half wrote about tasks they needed to do in the coming days. Half wrote about tasks they had already completed. The to-do list group fell asleep significantly faster. The more specific the list was, the faster they fell asleep.

The researchers suggest that writing tomorrow's tasks offloads them from active memory. Your brain has been holding onto them so you won't forget. Once they're written down, it can let go.

If you've ever lain awake thinking "I need to remember to..." over and over, this is the technique to try first.

Five minutes. Specific tasks. Not a reflection on the day, but a capture of what's ahead.

## 3. Give your body a physical reset

Once thoughts are on paper, a short physical routine gives your nervous system something to do besides rehearse tomorrow.

**Box breathing** is simple: breathe in for four counts, hold for four, breathe out for four, hold for four. Repeat for two to three minutes. The rhythm gives your mind a concrete anchor and slows the physical arousal that anxiety builds up.

**A body scan** works differently. Starting at your feet, you notice each part of your body without trying to relax it, just notice. Moving slowly upward. Most people find that attention and release tend to happen together without forcing it.

Neither of these will stop a thought spiral on their own. They work best after you've done something to address the thoughts themselves (writing, worry window, or the brain dump below).

## 4. Do a quick brain dump

A brain dump is different from a to-do list. It's less structured. You write whatever is in your head without organizing it, ten minutes, no agenda, no pressure to solve anything.

The goal is to move unfinished thinking from your head to the page so your brain stops cycling through it. You're not finding answers. You're just getting it out.

For nights when the worry is diffuse ("something feels off and I can't name it"), a brain dump often surfaces what's actually driving the anxiety. Once you can name it, it shrinks a little.

Keep your notebook by the bed. If you wake up at 3am with a thought, write it down and tell yourself you've captured it. That single habit removes a lot of middle-of-the-night vigilance.

## 5. Draw a hard line around your wind-down time

Worry tends to spike at bedtime partly because bedtime is when people finally open their email, catch up on news, or scroll social media. Those activities don't just bring in new information. They signal to your brain that active processing should continue.

Cutting off news and social media 45 to 60 minutes before sleep is one of the most consistently recommended sleep hygiene steps, and it's not arbitrary. The content you're consuming is worry-generating by design.

What works better in that window: something that asks low from you mentally. A conversation, a book that's absorbing but not stressful, a short walk, making tea. The goal isn't forced relaxation. It's just not actively adding new things to worry about.

## 6. Write a brief close for the day

Right before sleep, a two-to-three-minute written close gives the day a formal ending.

This isn't about reviewing everything that went wrong. It's closer to closing open browser tabs. One or two things that happened, one or two things you're setting aside until tomorrow.

This is where a [bedtime journal](/blog/bedtime-journal) becomes a genuine tool rather than a nice idea. The act of writing creates a small ritual signal: this is the end of today's active processing. What's written is held. You don't have to keep holding it yourself.

## 7. Notice when worrying is and isn't useful

Not all worry is avoidable, and some of it serves a purpose. Worry points toward something you care about. The question is whether the worry is actionable.

A useful frame: for every worry that surfaces at night, ask yourself one question. "Is there something I can do about this before I sleep?" Most of the time, the honest answer is no. Writing it down is the action. The rest waits.

For worries that are actionable (you genuinely forgot something important, you need to send a message), deal with it briefly. Write the message, set a reminder, make the note. Then close the loop.

The worries that feel urgent but aren't ("what if that project doesn't work out," "what if I said the wrong thing earlier") are the ones that benefit most from a written brain dump, a worry window, or simply the acknowledgment that there's nothing useful to do right now.

Distinguishing those two categories takes practice. Getting better at it is one of the slower but more durable ways to stop worrying taking over your nights. ## When to talk to someone

Occasional nighttime worry is normal. Some worry periods are longer, like a stressful work season or a family difficulty, and that's expected too.

But if you've been struggling to sleep most nights for three months or more, if the worry is affecting your ability to function during the day, or if anxiety feels out of control regardless of what you try, those are signs worth taking seriously.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia ([CBT-I](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/treatment/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-insomnia)) is the first-line treatment recommended for chronic insomnia. It's more effective than sleep medication over the long term, and it addresses the worry patterns that keep insomnia going. A GP or mental health professional can point you toward a provider or a structured digital program.

If you're experiencing persistent anxiety, panic attacks, or thoughts of self-harm, please talk to a doctor or mental health professional. These techniques support wellbeing; they don't replace care.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does worrying get worse at bedtime?

During the day, tasks and distractions interrupt worry before it builds. At night, the quiet removes those interruptions. Your brain also tends to release cortisol and adrenaline in response to anxiety, which raises alertness at the exact time you need to wind down.

### Does scheduling a daily worry time actually help?

Research suggests it can reduce the amount of time you spend worrying during the day, especially when combined with specific plans for when and how to use the worry window. However, a 2025 randomized controlled trial found it did not significantly improve sleep outcomes on its own. It works best as part of a broader approach rather than a single bedtime fix.

### What is the fastest thing to try for nighttime worrying?

A five-minute specific to-do list for tomorrow is the technique with the most direct sleep evidence. A 2018 polysomnography study found that people who wrote a detailed list of upcoming tasks fell asleep significantly faster than those who reflected on the day. The more specific the list, the larger the effect.

### How is worrying different from overthinking?

Worry tends to be future-focused: anticipating what might go wrong. Overthinking tends to be about replaying what has already happened or cycling through scenarios without resolution. The two often appear together at night. [Stopping the overthinking loop](/blog/how-to-stop-overthinking-at-night) requires some overlapping but slightly different tools.

### Can journaling make anxiety worse?

Open emotional venting at bedtime can sometimes sustain the spiral rather than close it. The techniques with better evidence are structured ones: writing tomorrow's tasks, a brief factual close of day, or prompts that help you name what you're feeling rather than amplify it. Saving open-ended expressive writing for earlier in the day is generally a safer bet.

### When should I see a doctor about anxiety or insomnia?

If sleep difficulties have persisted most nights for three months or longer, if your anxiety is affecting daily functioning, or if you're experiencing panic or thoughts of self-harm, talk to a doctor or mental health professional. CBT-I is the recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia. These self-help steps are a support, not a substitute for care.
