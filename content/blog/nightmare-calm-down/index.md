You wake at 3am and the dream is still there, vivid and wrong. Your heart is pounding, and you're trying to calm down after a nightmare that felt completely real. You know it wasn't, but your body hasn't gotten the message yet.

That gap between knowing and feeling is the hard part. The nightmare is over, but the alarm in your chest hasn't switched off. This post covers what to do in the first few minutes, how to ease back to sleep, and what to try if bad dreams keep coming back.

## Key takeaways

- The physical jolt from a nightmare (racing heart, tight chest) is a real nervous system response. It takes a few minutes to settle, and you can speed that up.
- Slow exhale breathing and sensory grounding are the fastest ways to calm down after a nightmare and return to the present.
- For recurring nightmares, a technique called imagery rehearsal therapy has solid research behind it and can be started at home.

## Why your body reacts so strongly

During REM sleep, the stage where most vivid dreaming happens, your brain's fear-processing regions are highly active. When a nightmare reaches a certain intensity, it can trigger the same physical alarm your body would have to a real threat: elevated heart rate, quickened breathing, muscle tension.

[The Sleep Foundation notes](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/nightmares/nightmare-disorder) that about 85% of adults have at least one nightmare a year. Most are tied to a stressful period and pass on their own. But a [2010 study in the journal Sleep](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2880244) found that 5.1% of adults have nightmares at least once a week.

Understanding that what you're feeling is a normal nervous system response, not a sign something is wrong with you, can itself take some of the edge off.

## The first two minutes: four things to do right now

These work best done in order. You don't need to do all of them perfectly.

**1. Let your exhale be longer than your inhale.** Extended exhales activate the parasympathetic nervous system, the part that tells your body it's safe to settle. Try breathing in for four counts and out for six or seven. No need to hold your breath or follow a strict pattern. The goal is simply to slow down the out-breath.

**2. Name where you are.** Out loud or in your head: your name, the room, the date. "I'm in my bedroom. It's Sunday. The dream was not real." This is an orienting step. It interrupts the emotional replay still running from the nightmare and anchors you in the present moment.

**3. Ground yourself with your senses.** Look around and name three things you can see. Feel the sheets or the pillow under your hand. Notice a sound in the room, even quiet. The 5-4-3-2-1 technique works by routing your attention out of the dream imagery and into your immediate environment, where the threat isn't. Five things you see, four you feel, three you hear, two you smell, one you taste.

**4. Keep the phone face-down.** Bright light and stimulation signal to your brain that it's time to wake up. If you need light, use a lamp on the lowest setting. Scrolling makes it harder to fall back asleep, and the content often doesn't help.

If you're still shaky after five minutes, that's within the normal range. A particularly vivid or emotionally loaded nightmare can leave a residue. You may need ten or fifteen minutes before the feeling fully passes.

## Getting back to sleep

Once your heart rate has come down, the goal is to ease your body back toward sleep without forcing it.

Stay in bed if you can. Getting up and doing something activating, like bright screens or intense thinking, can delay sleep by an hour or more. Instead:

- Keep the room dark and cool
- Play a low-level ambient sound (rain, white noise, soft music) to give your ears something neutral to settle on
- Do a slow body scan: starting at your feet, deliberately relax each muscle group upward

If you've been lying awake for more than twenty minutes and feel frustrated rather than drowsy, it's fine to get up briefly. Sit somewhere dim, read something undemanding, then return when you feel sleepy again. Lying awake tense trains your brain to associate your bed with wakefulness, which can make the next night harder.

## Should you write the nightmare down?

For occasional bad dreams, writing or speaking the nightmare out can help you process it and discharge some of its emotional weight. Getting it out of your head and into words makes it feel smaller for a lot of people. You can use meow note's voice journaling to quietly talk through what happened while soft ambient sounds play, then write a calmer version of the ending in the text journal.

For recurring nightmares, there's a specific technique where journaling the nightmare and deliberately rewriting its ending can reduce how often it comes back (covered in the next section).

One caveat: if replaying the nightmare in detail increases your distress rather than reducing it, stop. Not everyone finds recall helpful, especially when the nightmare is linked to something difficult. Give yourself permission to let it fade without examining it.

For more on using a journal to improve sleep, see [journaling for sleep](/blog/journaling-for-sleep).

## Why nightmares keep coming back

Occasional nightmares usually reflect something going on in your life. Common triggers:

- **High stress or anxiety**, especially unresolved worry that follows you to bed
- **Sleep deprivation**, which intensifies emotional processing during dreams
- **Alcohol**, which disrupts REM sleep and can cause more vivid or unpleasant dreams as it metabolizes overnight
- **Certain medications**, including some antidepressants, beta-blockers, and medications for Parkinson's disease
- **Traumatic experiences**, where nightmares are often part of how the brain processes what happened

If your nightmares started around the same time as a new medication, mention it to whoever prescribed it. There may be an alternative.

If they began after something difficult happened and they keep returning, that's a signal worth taking seriously. See "When to talk to someone" below.

## A longer-term approach: nightmare rescripting

For recurring nightmares not linked to a current trauma, imagery rehearsal therapy (IRT) is a well-supported option you can try on your own. [A guide by Psyche](https://psyche.co/guides/how-to-use-relaxation-and-rescripting-to-calm-your-nightmares) describes it as the first-line treatment for nightmare disorder, and it is recommended in the [AASM Best Practice Guide for nightmare disorder](https://aasm.org/resources/bestpracticeguides/nightmaredisorder.pdf).

The idea is straightforward: you choose one recurring nightmare, write down how it unfolds, and then deliberately rewrite it with a different ending. You don't need to make it happy. You just need to change it in some meaningful way. Then you spend ten to fifteen minutes each day imagining the new version.

Most people notice some change within one to two weeks. Clinically significant improvement typically takes up to four weeks of daily practice.

A simplified version to try:

1. Write the nightmare in present tense, exactly as it happens.
2. Identify the moment where it turns worst.
3. Write a new version from that point, with a different outcome. Make it specific and visual.
4. Read the new ending every night before sleep for two to four weeks.

If the nightmare is linked to a specific traumatic event, work with a therapist rather than doing this alone. Trauma-linked nightmares respond best to trauma-informed care.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you calm down after a nightmare fast?

The fastest combination is extended-exhale breathing (out-breath longer than in-breath) paired with sensory grounding: naming what you can see and feel in the room right now. Both can bring your heart rate down within a few minutes for most people.

### Why does my heart race after a nightmare?

During REM sleep, the brain's fear-processing circuits are active. A vivid or threatening nightmare can trigger the same physical stress response your body would have to a real danger: heart rate spikes, breathing quickens, muscles tense. Once you're awake and your brain registers you're safe, the response settles on its own. Slow breathing helps it settle faster.

### Should I tell someone about my nightmare?

You don't have to. For most bad dreams, the most useful step is grounding yourself and getting back to sleep. If a nightmare stays with you the next day, talking it through briefly can help. For recurring nightmares, a therapist, especially one familiar with sleep or trauma, is worth seeing.

### Can nightmares affect you the next day?

Yes. A particularly vivid nightmare can leave emotional residue even after you've gone back to sleep. Some people feel anxious, flat, or unusually tired the morning after a bad dream. The effect tends to ease by midday, especially if you got enough total sleep.

### What causes the same nightmare to repeat?

Recurring nightmares are often a sign that something emotional hasn't been fully processed. Stress, anxiety, and past difficult experiences are the most common causes. For recurring nightmares linked to trauma, imagery rehearsal therapy with professional support is the recommended approach. For stress-linked ones, addressing the underlying stressor and improving overall sleep hygiene often helps over several weeks.

### Is it normal to be scared to go back to sleep after a nightmare?

Yes, and it is a common cycle: the nightmare makes you scared to sleep, poor sleep makes dreams more intense, and so on. The post on [being afraid to go to sleep](/blog/afraid-to-go-to-sleep) covers ways to break the pattern.

## When to talk to someone

Occasional nightmares are a normal part of life. See a doctor or mental health professional if:

- Nightmares are happening multiple times a week and disrupting your sleep
- You're avoiding sleep because of them
- They began after a traumatic event or feature it directly
- You're experiencing daytime anxiety, flashbacks, or low mood alongside the nightmares
- You have thoughts of self-harm

Recurring nightmares are one of the markers of PTSD and are very treatable. [Imagery rehearsal therapy and other CBT-based approaches](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9013762/) are the recommended first-line treatments, and a trauma-informed therapist can guide you through them safely.
