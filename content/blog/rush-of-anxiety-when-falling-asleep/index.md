You're finally horizontal. The room is dark. Your body starts to loosen. And then it happens: a rush of anxiety when falling asleep, sharp enough to yank you back to wide-awake, heart thumping, staring at the ceiling.

Nothing is wrong with you. What you're experiencing has a name, a cause, and some approaches that actually help. Here's what to know.

## Key takeaways

- The rush of anxiety often happens during the hypnagogic state, the transitional window between wakefulness and sleep, when suppressed stress tends to resurface.
- As cortisol drops in the evening, your capacity to manage anxious thoughts drops with it, making the bedtime hour a genuinely vulnerable window.
- Writing out your unfinished tasks before bed (specifically tasks, not feelings) has been shown in a controlled sleep lab study to help people fall asleep faster.

## What that rush actually is

Between wakefulness and sleep, there's a brief transitional period called the hypnagogic state. During it, your muscle tone loosens, your brain waves slow, and your mind begins the loose drifting that precedes dreams.

This is also when suppressed thoughts tend to surface. A [2022 review in the Journal of Sleep Research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10078162/) found that both anxiety and depression were associated with increased hypnagogic experiences, and that thoughts people had pushed aside during the day were more likely to reappear at sleep onset.

So that sudden wave of dread is not random. It's the noise you kept at bay all day, catching up with you the moment your mind's defenses go quiet.

Some people also notice hypnic jerks during this window: involuntary full-body twitches that can feel alarming but are harmless on their own. When a jerk is followed by a wave of anxiety, the anxiety was often already building before the jolt arrived.

## Why it hits hardest at this exact moment

Three things converge at bedtime to make anxiety feel worse.

**Cortisol drops.** Your body's main stress-regulating hormone falls in the evening by design, because high cortisol would prevent sleep. But the dip also leaves you with less capacity to regulate anxious thoughts, according to the [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/mental-health/anxiety-at-night).

**Distraction disappears.** Tasks, conversations, and small decisions keep your mind occupied during the day. Lying in the dark with nothing to do, your brain turns toward whatever you'd been pushing aside: the email left unanswered, the conversation you replayed twice, the appointment you haven't scheduled.

**The nervous system is already activated.** If you carry stress through the day, your baseline arousal is elevated by the time you lie down. Research published in [Sleep Science (2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10157827/) found that people with more severe insomnia showed higher morning cortisol and higher tension-anxiety scores, supporting what's known as the hyperarousal model: the nervous system stuck in a state of readiness when it should be winding down.

## The loop that keeps it going

Anxiety disrupts sleep. Poor sleep makes you more anxious. It is not a complicated spiral, but it is a sticky one.

When sleep is fragmented by anxiety, you get less deep sleep and less REM sleep, both of which help regulate mood and stress response, according to the [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/mental-health/anxiety-at-night). The next day you're more reactive, more easily overwhelmed, and more likely to lie awake again that night. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21543-sleep-anxiety) notes that anxiety is the most common mental health disorder in the U.S., affecting around 40 million people, and most of them experience some form of sleep disruption alongside it.

The goal isn't to break the loop in one night. It's to lower your evening arousal level, consistently, until the cycle starts to loosen. If [overthinking at night](/blog/how-to-stop-overthinking-at-night) is part of what's feeding the anxiety, that post covers techniques specific to the rumination side.

## Six things to try tonight

### 1. Write your tomorrow list before you lie down

This is specific: not journaling about your day, but writing out the tasks you haven't finished yet. A [2018 study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29058942/) had 57 adults write either a to-do list or a completed-tasks list before sleeping in a sleep lab. The to-do list writers fell asleep an average of nine minutes faster. The more specific the list ("email Maya re: Thursday" rather than "deal with work"), the faster they fell asleep.

The mechanism is offloading: your brain holds open loops, and writing them down tells it those loops are safe to release for the night. A detailed [journaling for sleep](/blog/journaling-for-sleep) routine can incorporate this and build on it.

### 2. Try the physiological sigh

Two sharp inhales through the nose (the second one tops off the lungs), then one long exhale through the mouth. Repeat two or three times. The double inhale fills the lungs more completely, and the long exhale is what slows the heart rate by activating the parasympathetic nervous system. It doesn't fix the underlying anxiety, but it can interrupt the physical spiral in the moment.

### 3. A quick body scan from the feet up

Tense each muscle group for five seconds, then release. Start at your feet and move upward. The value is in the contrast: noticing the difference between tension and release helps your nervous system recognize what relaxation actually feels like. Many people do this while already in bed. It takes about ten minutes.

### 4. Move your worry window earlier

Worrying at bedtime isn't something you can simply decide to stop. But you can redirect it. Set a 20-minute slot earlier in the evening (6 or 7 PM works for many people) as the designated time for anxious thoughts. Write them down, think them through, give them their due. When anxiety surfaces at bedtime, you have somewhere to put it: "I'll think about this tomorrow at 7. Not now." The boundary takes practice to hold, but tends to get easier over a few weeks.

### 5. Give your mind something quiet to hold

Trying to empty your mind completely is difficult to do on command. It works better to give it something low-stakes: counting backward from 300 in threes, a slow mental walk through a familiar place, a specific absorbing memory. These work by giving the brain's pattern-seeking tendencies something to do that isn't catastrophizing.

### 6. Talk it out before the lights go off

Sometimes the anxiety isn't formed enough to write as a list. It's a general unease or a pile of half-thoughts. Speaking them out loud, even to yourself, can help externalize what's circling in your head.

[meow note](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6776287385) is a voice bedtime journal: speak the day out loud while calm sleep ambience plays, and it tracks your nightly mood over time so you can start to spot which kinds of days tend to bring on the anxious nights. Talking is lower-effort than writing when you're already exhausted, and keeping it separate from your bed helps your body stop associating the mattress with mental noise. If you want prompt ideas to give the reflection more shape, [bedtime journal prompts](/blog/bedtime-journal-prompts) has 35 you can draw from.

## When to talk to someone

The strategies above help with ordinary pre-sleep anxiety. If any of the following are true for you, please talk to a doctor or a mental health professional:

- The anxiety at sleep onset has been happening most nights for three months or more
- You wake in the night with a pounding heart or intense fear (possible nocturnal panic attacks)
- The sleep loss is affecting your work, concentration, or ability to get through the day
- You have thoughts of self-harm

[Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/treatment/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-insomnia) is the first-line, evidence-based treatment for chronic insomnia. It directly addresses the thoughts and behaviors that feed the anxiety-sleep cycle. A typical course runs four to eight sessions. Your doctor can refer you, or a therapist trained in CBT-I can take you through it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do I get a rush of anxiety right when I'm about to fall asleep?

The hypnagogic state, the brief transition between waking and sleep, is when your brain shifts from active processing to rest mode. Thoughts that were suppressed during the day tend to resurface here, and your cortisol is low, which reduces your capacity to regulate them. Combine that with a nervous system that may already be running hot from the day, and the result is an anxiety wave right at the threshold of sleep.

### Is this the same as a nocturnal panic attack?

Not necessarily. A rush of anxiety when falling asleep usually keeps you from drifting off in the first place. Nocturnal panic attacks typically jolt you out of sleep with intense fear, rapid heartbeat, sweating and shortness of breath, often without a clear trigger. Both are worth discussing with a doctor if they happen regularly.

### Will journaling before bed make the anxiety worse?

It depends on the type. Writing about your emotions in detail immediately before sleep can increase arousal for some people. Writing a concrete to-do list, though, has been shown in a sleep lab study to reduce how long it takes to fall asleep. If expressive writing feels activating at bedtime, try shifting it to earlier in the evening and using the pre-sleep window for a simple task list or voice note instead.

### How long before techniques start to help?

Breathing exercises and body scans can help in the same session. The to-do list method produces an immediate effect for many people. Building a consistent worry window and evening routine usually takes a few weeks before it starts to feel automatic. CBT-I produces meaningful results for most people within four to eight sessions.

### Can pre-sleep anxiety become conditioned over time?

Yes. When anxiety repeatedly arrives at bedtime, the bed itself can start to function as a trigger, a process called conditioned arousal. You may notice you feel calmer anywhere except your own bed. CBT-I specifically targets this by rebuilding the association between bed and sleep, usually through sleep restriction and stimulus control techniques.

### What should I do if the anxiety feels physical, like a racing heart?

First, acknowledge it rather than fight it. The more you try to suppress physical anxiety sensations, the more your brain treats them as a threat. A physiological sigh or body scan can help move your nervous system toward rest. If a racing heart at bedtime happens regularly, tell your doctor: in rare cases, hyperthyroidism and other physical conditions can cause both anxiety and insomnia.
