You have had bad nights before. But this feels different. It has been weeks of lying awake watching the minutes tick by, or waking at 3am with your heart already going, or dragging through every afternoon unable to think straight. At some point you start to wonder: how do I tell if I have insomnia, or is this just a rough patch that will pass?

It is worth knowing the answer. Insomnia is a real, diagnosable sleep disorder with specific clinical criteria that separate it from a bad week. Knowing how to tell if you have insomnia means you can stop second-guessing yourself and actually do something about it.

Here is what those patterns look like.

## Key takeaways
- Insomnia requires two things at once: a sleep problem at night AND real impairment during the day, not just tiredness.
- The clinical threshold for chronic insomnia is three or more nights per week for at least three months, per [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/diagnosis).
- The most effective treatment is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which helps [70 to 80% of people](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia) who complete it.

## What insomnia actually means

Most people assume insomnia just means you cannot sleep. Clinically, it is more specific.

Insomnia is a sleep disorder that [affects 10 to 30% of adults](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/diagnosis). To meet the diagnostic standard (DSM-5, the reference used by doctors and sleep specialists), you need both of these at the same time:

**A nighttime sleep problem** (at least one):
- Trouble falling asleep (most guidelines flag more than 30 minutes as a meaningful threshold)
- Waking repeatedly during the night and struggling to get back to sleep
- Waking up earlier than you want to and lying there unable to drift off again
- Dreading bedtime and resisting a reasonable bedtime hour

**A daytime cost** (at least one):
- Fatigue or feeling washed out, even after enough hours in bed
- Trouble concentrating, remembering things, or making decisions
- Impaired performance at work, in relationships, or at school
- Irritability, low motivation, or feeling emotionally fragile
- Excessive sleepiness during the day, or paradoxically, feeling wired and unable to rest

The daytime impairment is not a side effect. It is part of the diagnosis. If you sleep badly but feel completely fine the next day, that does not meet the criteria. Both halves need to be present.

## The difference between a bad week and chronic insomnia

A few nights of poor sleep after a stressful event is not insomnia. Clock-watching the night before a job interview is not insomnia. The pattern has to repeat.

**Acute insomnia** follows the same nighttime-plus-daytime pattern but lasts less than three months. It is often tied to a specific trigger: a job loss, a difficult relationship, a health scare. For many people it resolves on its own once the stressor passes.

**Chronic insomnia** occurs at least three nights per week for three months or more, even with adequate time in bed and a reasonable sleep environment. At that point, it typically will not go away on its own.

The three-month mark matters because it separates an adjustment period from something that has taken on a life of its own. If you have been lying awake most nights since spring and it is now autumn, that is not a rough patch.

## Signs you might be brushing off

Some insomnia symptoms are obvious. Others get explained away.

**Dreading bedtime.** If you start feeling anxious or restless as the evening arrives, not tired but wary, that is a real signal. The bed has started to feel like a place where wakefulness happens.

**Waking up tired after a full night.** You were in bed eight hours but feel like you barely slept. That unrefreshing, heavy-limbed morning is part of the insomnia picture, not just "not being a morning person."

**Sleep anxiety during the day.** Thinking about tonight's sleep before lunch. Checking the clock to calculate how many hours you have left. Planning your day around your sleep deficit. When sleep becomes the thing you are managing, you are past a rough patch.

**Relying on something to fall asleep.** Alcohol, antihistamines, or an hour of scrolling just to get drowsy. These often worsen underlying insomnia over time, and the reliance itself is a sign that sleep is not happening naturally.

## What insomnia is not

Getting the distinction right matters, because a few other conditions look similar from the inside.

**Sleep apnea** interrupts breathing during sleep, causing repeated micro-arousals. If you snore loudly, wake up gasping, or a partner notices you stopping breathing, apnea is more likely than insomnia. A sleep study diagnoses it; insomnia does not require a sleep study.

**Restless legs syndrome** creates an uncomfortable urge to move your legs at night, making it hard to stay still enough to sleep. The physical sensation is the key difference from insomnia.

**A circadian rhythm problem** (common in shift workers or frequent travellers) means your internal clock is misaligned, not that sleep itself is broken. Adjusting the schedule often fixes the sleep.

**Normal short-term adjustment.** A few weeks of poor sleep after a major life change is an expected human response. It becomes insomnia when the pattern outlasts the trigger.

## What helps once you recognize it

The most effective approach to insomnia does not start with sleeping pills.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the [recommended first-line treatment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42539544/) for both acute and chronic insomnia. A [2026 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42813493/) found statistically significant improvements in sleep efficiency and time awake after waking. [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia) puts the improvement rate at 70 to 80% of people who complete it, typically across six to eight weekly sessions.

CBT-I works by addressing the thought patterns and behaviors built up around sleep, not just the symptoms on a given night. Stimulus control (getting out of bed when you cannot sleep), sleep restriction (temporarily narrowing your sleep window), and relaxation techniques are its main tools.

One small thing that helps alongside that: clearing the mental queue before you lie down. The worry and replaying that worsen insomnia tend to happen in bed, where there is nothing else to do. Moving that processing earlier in the evening, with a quick voice or written brain dump in an app like [meow note](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6776287385), gives the mind somewhere to put the day before the pressure of falling asleep starts.

If [worrying before bed](/blog/how-to-stop-worrying) is a bigger part of what is happening, that post covers worry postponement and a few practical techniques worth trying.

## When to talk to someone

If you recognize the pattern here (three or more nights a week, daytime fog affecting how you function, sleep dread settled in as a background hum), it is worth talking to a doctor.

Insomnia that has lasted three months or more and is affecting your work, relationships, or daily functioning is not a willpower problem. A GP can rule out underlying medical causes and refer you to a CBT-I therapist or sleep specialist. The [Sleep Foundation's treatment guide](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/treatment) is a clear starting point for what to expect.

If you are also experiencing panic attacks, persistent low mood, or any thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out now rather than waiting. A doctor or mental health professional is the right person to start with, and these deserve their own support, separate from the sleep work.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many nights of bad sleep before it counts as insomnia?

The clinical standard for chronic insomnia is at least three nights per week for at least three months. Occasional poor nights, even several in a row during a stressful period, do not meet that bar. What matters is the repeating pattern over time and whether your days are consistently suffering.

### Can I have insomnia if I do eventually fall asleep?

Yes. Taking 45 minutes to an hour to fall asleep most nights counts as a sleep-initiation problem, even if you do eventually drift off. Waking in the night and spending a total of 30 or more extra minutes awake also qualifies. Getting there eventually does not rule it out.

### Is insomnia the same as sleep anxiety?

They overlap but are not identical. Sleep anxiety (dread and worry specifically about sleep) often causes insomnia, and chronic insomnia frequently produces sleep anxiety as the brain learns to pair the bed with wakefulness. Many people end up with both. CBT-I addresses the anxiety component directly.

### What is the difference between acute and chronic insomnia?

Acute insomnia lasts less than three months and is often tied to an identifiable stressor. Chronic insomnia persists for three months or more, at least three nights per week. Both share the nighttime-plus-daytime pattern. If you are [lying awake at night and just cannot sleep](/blog/cant-sleep), the acute-versus-chronic distinction helps determine how urgently to seek support.

### Does diagnosing insomnia require a sleep study?

No. Insomnia is usually diagnosed through a clinical interview, a sleep diary, and sometimes a questionnaire like the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. A sleep study (polysomnography) is used to rule out other disorders like sleep apnea but is not needed to confirm insomnia itself.

### What about Sunday night insomnia specifically?

[Sunday night insomnia](/blog/sunday-night-insomnia) is a recognized pattern: difficulty sleeping on Sunday nights even when other nights are fine, often driven by anticipation of Monday. If it is genuinely only Sundays, addressing the trigger usually helps. If it has spread to most nights, the full diagnostic picture applies.
